What this is

Not prompts.
Written procedures that execute.

I own end-to-end finance operations for a B2B unit of roughly 2,500 active enterprise accounts — forecasting, aging analysis, recovery strategy and the automation behind all of it. This portfolio covers five processes I automated, two of which grew past a single procedure into systems with persistent state.

Each tool is a precise written procedure — data source, field names, routing rules, exceptions, and what to do when something is missing — that an AI agent executes end to end. If a new analyst read the document, they could do the job. That's the test I hold each one to.

The value isn't speed. It's that the knowledge stopped living in my head, the arithmetic is proven before anything is produced, and every decision that could reach a customer still passes through a person.

Why a finance team should care: most automation risk isn't bad math — it's confident math on quietly incomplete data. Every tool here is built to distrust its own inputs and fail loudly instead of producing a plausible number.

A note on the figures. Everything in this portfolio is anonymised: no client identities, no colleague names, no internal system or table names, no ticket references. Amounts are expressed as ranges or proportions, and the interface examples use synthetic data. The structures, validation logic and governance rules shown are the real ones.

The work

Five processes, in production

Each one replaced a recurring manual job. Read the green row first — that's the outcome.

Case 01

Monthly AR Communications

Cadence Monthly
Scale 100+ enterprise accounts
Volume ~1,100 invoice rows / run
Type Multi-skill system
Problem
Every month, one collection email per forecast account: pull the aging, choose the template that matches the account's collection status, paste the invoice table by hand, clean a messy recipient list, and remember from memory which accounts must never be contacted. A week of the month — and one copy-paste error reaches a customer.
What I built
A three-stage pipeline. One stage queries the data warehouse and produces an invoice-level dataset, replacing the manual export entirely. The next routes every collection status to its correct template, resolves recipients through overrides, list hygiene and a stale-contact check against real correspondence history, and renders the invoice table from the data rather than pasting it. Account totals are derived from the invoice detail in the same query, so the balance summary and the itemised table are structurally incapable of disagreeing.
Guardrail
Balances must reconcile to the cent per account before a draft exists. Accounts with a net credit balance are never chased. Internal colleague addresses are automatically stripped from customer-facing mail. Every account deliberately not contacted appears in an internal exceptions report with the reason.
Outcome
100+ accounts contacted in a single sitting with the invoice detail already rendered and reconciled. The first week of the month moved from copy-paste to disputes, payment plans and negotiations — the work that actually moves cash.
Case 02

Forecast Tracking

Cadence Continuous
Scope ~50 accounts / cycle
Depth 13 blocker categories
Type System + live dashboard
Problem
A spreadsheet can tell you an account is still open. It can never tell you why — whether the invoice was never received, the money is already ours and a credit was never applied, or the payment arrived and was misapplied internally. Those three cases require completely different actions, and two of them shouldn't involve contacting the customer at all.
What I built
A tracker that reads the money from a filtered mirror of the monthly forecast, classifies every account against a tolerance band, then reads the customer's actual email replies and classifies the blocker into one of 13 categories — each with a plain-language summary, the customer's verbatim sentence and a link to the thread. It detects unapplied credits by matching open credit amounts against the outstanding balance, snapshots every run so the trajectory is visible rather than just today's photo, and surfaces it all in a live dashboard.
Guardrail
It never sends and never drafts on its own — follow-up drafts exist only when I click the button for that account, and the button disables itself once used so no customer is chased twice. It refuses to publish a report when the source read looks incomplete, and it states which month the figures belong to before showing a single number.
Outcome
Open accounts moved from "still unpaid" to a named, evidenced reason. Collection effort now goes only where the customer is actually the blocker — unapplied credits route to cash application instead of triggering a dunning email.
Case 03

Weekly Deactivation Report

Cadence Weekly
Scale ~2,500 accounts screened
Output Two grouped reports
Type Skill + persistent state
Problem
Two weekly reports asking account owners to authorise deactivation of the worst-paying accounts, ranked by 60+ day exposure and grouped by owner. Rebuilding them by hand meant re-deriving the ranking, re-typing last week's commentary, and losing any sense of what changed.
What I built
A weekly run that screens the full portfolio, splits it into two segments using an owner classifier, ranks and groups the top accounts per segment, and produces both reports plus a run log. The commentary column is the interesting part: I fill it in inside the draft, so the following week the tool reads last week's own email to recover it. Because the source data is a daily full refresh with no history, every source file is archived and every run snapshotted — otherwise the week-over-week comparison, which is the actual value of the report, would be impossible.
Guardrail
Commentary is never invented — a new account shows an empty cell, and that's the honest answer. If an account's owner isn't already known, the run stops and asks rather than guessing a segment. It also reports gross exposure alongside the net figure, and flags cases where a sibling entity of the same client holds an offsetting credit, so the deactivation request is defensible before it's sent.
Outcome
A weekly report nobody has to rebuild, with prior commentary carried forward automatically, week-over-week movement visible, and contradictions between commentary and account status flagged rather than silently inherited.
Case 04

Ticket Triage & Distribution

Cadence Scheduled weekly
Taxonomy 13 request types
Team Routes across 4 analysts
Type Scheduled agent
Problem
A support queue that aged quietly. Every ticket older than five days needed to be read, understood, classified and either resolved or handed to the right person — and the subject line is close to useless, because most tickets are automated notifications the customer replied to.
What I built
A scheduled run that classifies each ticket by reading the message body after stripping our own automated boilerplate — bank details in a system footer are not a customer asking about bank details. It resolves 13 request types with sub-types for payment and remittance cases, routes my own portfolio accounts back to me, distributes the rest evenly across the team oldest-first, and produces a colour-coded workbook with a suggested resolution comment already drafted per type. Because the raw payload is far too large to process in one pass, the work is split across parallel workers whose outputs reconcile against the total.
Guardrail
It is scheduled, but a scheduled run prepares everything and stops. It never closes a ticket, reassigns it or posts a comment without my explicit approval — and payment claims always carry a "verify in the ledger first" flag, because a payment that looks received is not the same as a payment correctly allocated.
Outcome
The queue stopped aging. Every ticket over five days old is read, classified and routed the same day each week, distributed evenly instead of piling on one person — and I review a single report instead of opening thirty tickets.
Case 05

Manual Tax Invoices — Australia

Cadence On demand
Profile Low volume, high stakes
Scope AU GST compliance
Type Skill
Problem
Manual tax invoices for Australian customers, where the tax treatment has to be correct before the document leaves. Low volume means it's never fresh in your head — the exact profile where manual work fails: infrequent, detailed, unforgiving. A tax error here isn't an internal annoyance; it's a correction, a credit and rebill, and a credibility cost with the customer.
What I built
The rule set written down once — how the taxable amount is derived, how tax is applied and rounded, what must appear on a compliant document — then executed the same way every time, producing a complete invoice ready for review.
Guardrail
Two hard stops. The arithmetic must close before anything renders: line items sum to the subtotal, tax equals the correct rate on that subtotal, subtotal plus tax equals the total. And the customer's tax registration number is mandatory — leave it blank and the tool produces no document at all, with no override.
Outcome
A whole class of error removed. The tax is computed rather than remembered, and the corrections and customer-facing apologies that follow a bad tax figure simply don't occur.
Also delivered

AI Voice Agent for High-Volume Collections

Role AI Integration Analyst
Segment High-volume, low-balance
Type Vendor AI deployment
Problem
The long tail of the portfolio — many accounts, small balances — is uneconomical to work by phone one at a time, yet ignoring it lets small balances age indefinitely.
What I did
Deployed an AI voice agent (VOXIA) to handle outbound collection calls for that segment as a formal AI Integration Analyst, integrated with the ticketing system so every call outcome created a traceable follow-up and a clear escalation path back to a human collector.
Outcome
Coverage extended to a segment that previously received almost no direct contact, without adding headcount — and with the escalation path designed in from the start rather than bolted on.

How I build

The method, in seven steps

I never started from "what can AI do?". I started from my own calendar, and worked backwards.

STEP 01

Define the problem and the goal

Find the task that costs the most time or repeats the most. Define what a successful result looks like. Solve one problem before expanding.

STEP 02

Map the manual process

Write down how the job is done today, with no AI at all. Name the decision made at each step, and the inputs and outputs at each end.

STEP 03

Decide where AI belongs

"Copy A into B" is plain automation. "Read, summarise, classify, decide, write" is where a model earns its place.

STEP 04

Design the data flow

What triggers the run, what exact instruction the model executes on that data, and where the result is allowed to land.

STEP 05

Prototype and test the extremes

Easy cases, hard cases and broken cases. Adjust until the output is consistent, not until it works once.

STEP 06

Put a human in the loop

Build the pause in deliberately: the result stops for approval before it reaches a customer. Never bolt this on afterwards.

STEP 07

Turn every correction into a written rule

Each correction becomes a permanent rule with the real case attached — the account, the date, the number. The tools get more correct with use instead of decaying.

What this actually produced: two of the five processes outgrew being a single procedure. The moment I needed to know what happened last month, a stateless tool wasn't enough — so they became systems where one stage writes a ledger another reads a month later, with run snapshots and archived source data behind them. Collections is a month-over-month job; most of the work eventually needs that shape.

Governance

The limits I set, written down

A tool that stops and asks is worth more than one that's confidently wrong. These limits are where professional judgment lives — the automation runs up to them and no further.

✋
Nothing customer-facing sends itselfall five
Every email, invoice and ticket resolution waits for a human. The tools prepare; a person decides.
🚫
Never chase a credit balanceAR comms
If an account is net negative, no collection email goes out regardless of its status. One account carried a six-figure net credit — asking them to pay would have been simply wrong.
🔒
Some accounts are never contacted automatically — and are still reported every monthAR comms
A small set of accounts is excluded from automated outreach by standing instruction. They appear in the internal report even when everything is fine, because a watchlist that only speaks up when something breaks is one you stop trusting.
✍️
Never invent commentary — blank stays blankweekly report
Analyst commentary on an account is human work. A new account shows an empty cell, which is more useful than a plausible sentence nobody wrote.
🙋
Never guess a classification — stop and askweekly report
If an account's owner isn't already known, the run pauses instead of dropping it into whichever segment looks likely. A wrong segment sends a deactivation request to the wrong person.
⏸️
A scheduled run prepares and stopsticket triage
It executes on its own every week, but never closes, reassigns or comments without explicit approval — and never treats a payment as resolved without ledger verification first.
🧾
No customer tax number, no documenttax invoices
A missing registration number produces nothing at all, not a warning. And the arithmetic must close before the invoice renders.
📉

If a source read looks incomplete or the totals don't reconcile, the tool refuses to deliver and explains why. No figure reaches a stakeholder unless it can be traced back to its source.

"These limits aren't friction — they're the reason the output is trustworthy enough to put in front of a customer or a controller. Deciding them in advance, and writing them down so they can't drift, is the part of the job automation can't do for us."

Engineering judgment

Things I only learned by being wrong first

Each of these is now a permanent rule in the relevant procedure. All figures anonymised.

01

A truncated read looks like a report

Reading a large source directly returned roughly one row out of fifty, with no error. Nothing announced that data was missing. The tools now read a filtered mirror and abort when the row count is implausible.

02

Summary views can quietly overstate

A pre-built summary tab inflated attainment by more than thirty percentage points because it mixed in cash outside my scope. The verification step is now inverted on purpose: if my totals match that view, I read the wrong one.

03

Don't trust a pre-computed age field

A warehouse aging column was calculated at period close, not as of today — six weeks stale across every row. Deriving the age from the due date instead reproduced the correct value exactly across all 1,000+ documents.

04

Reconcile a new pipeline before trusting it

When replacing a manual export with a direct query, I diffed the two across 101 accounts and roughly $20M in open AR: 25 of 27 fields matched exactly, totals matched to the cent, and the single known difference is documented and explained.

05

Compute cost is a design choice

The same query took over 100 seconds on one warehouse configuration and 6 seconds on another. Knowing which to use turned a process that "hung" into one that runs while you wait.

06

Beating a target isn't automatically good news

In one month, roughly a third of an over-collection came from accounts forecast at zero. That's a forecasting signal, not a collections win — and naming the split is the difference between a real answer and a vanity number.
